**Circle by Swasth — product understanding and implementation audit**

25 September 2026 · Source snapshot `b556c9f` · Audit only; application code and dependencies were not changed.

**Circle is a family care coordination product for people living across countries.** The intended first audience was confirmed by the founder during this audit. The implemented scenario is an adult child in Sydney coordinating health and care for parents and a grandmother in New Delhi. This India–Australia corridor is a concrete prototype scenario; expansion to other countries has not been defined.

The core promise is: **“Help me know what changed, understand what needs attention, arrange the next step, and know that someone completed it—even when I am far away.”** Reliable information and shared responsibility are the main sources of value. Records, AI explanations, reminders and professional bookings should all support that promise.

Today Circle is a substantial interactive demonstration with a coherent visual system and two distinct account experiences. It can support guided product discussions. It cannot yet support real family coordination across devices: its information and mutations live in a single running app session, and many confirmations represent a simulation rather than a completed external action.

**Who the product serves.** These roles have different needs and should remain distinct in the product and permissions model.

| Person | Evidence and present implementation | Job Circle needs to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family organiser abroad | Arjun, 23, Sydney; “Family Organiser” and “Care Captain”; family dashboard and administration | See a trustworthy picture of home, delegate work, arrange care, and reduce repeated checking and uncertainty |
| Parent managing an ongoing condition | Rajiv, 57, diabetes and hypertension; Neha, 52, thyroid and cholesterol concerns | Keep control of personal information, understand the plan, record meaningful updates, and receive appropriate practical support |
| Older family member | Savita, 76, reduced mobility; dedicated Home, AI, Care, booking and settings presentations | Understand the next action, finish it with minimal effort, ask for help, and know what is shared |
| Local helper or second organiser | Implied by task coordination; no distinct account or delegation model | Accept an assignment, acknowledge responsibility, report completion, and handle a missed handoff |
| Care professional | 36 seeded profiles across nine categories; no professional application or operational workflow | Receive authorised context, confirm availability, deliver the appointment and record agreed follow-up |
| Family decision-maker/payer | “Circle Family” subscription and payment mockups | Understand value, costs, payment responsibility and service reliability; willingness to pay remains unvalidated |

The organiser is the strongest candidate for the initial acquisition customer and likely payer. That is a product hypothesis, not established customer research. Parents and grandparents are participating adults with their own choices, rather than records owned by the organiser. Some families will need assisted or proxy use; age alone must not determine autonomy or permissions.

The current prototype supports only Arjun and Savita as switchable accounts. Rajiv and Neha are family profiles, not independently implemented account experiences. Actual invitations, acceptance, identity verification and consent are missing. The hard-coded four-person family is useful demonstration data, but it is not yet a general household model.

**The emotional and practical problem.** Distance makes small uncertainties accumulate: whether a medicine was recorded, whether a report was understood, who will prepare for a visit, whether someone needs help, and whether an appointment actually happened. A useful Circle should reduce repeated calls and duplicated work while preserving the recipient’s dignity. Reassurance should come from an attributable update with a timestamp and a completed action. “All up to date” without evidence is the wrong foundation for this promise.

A recommended core flow is: a family member adds a report or observation → Circle shows the source and what changed → a person chooses the next action → someone accepts responsibility → completion is recorded → authorised family members receive the update. The present app has screens for much of this flow, but the links between these steps are incomplete.

**What is worth preserving.** The warm palette, consistent typography, family imagery, reusable components and restrained tone give the product an identifiable foundation. The organiser/personal-account distinction is valuable. Savita’s plain verbs, larger controls, completion feedback and family-help affordance demonstrate useful intent. Account switching, local task mutations, record filters and booking state changes provide more than a static mockup. Some flows explicitly disclose their limitations, especially organiser payment, record sharing and consultation simulation. Strict TypeScript and successful platform exports are a useful engineering base.

**Architecture assessment.** Expo Router, TypeScript and the existing component system are appropriate foundations to continue from. The primary problem is data ownership and domain modelling. A single Context provider holds most mutable entities, while imported seed arrays and hard-coded prose serve as competing representations of the same facts. Large route components mix layout, business decisions and mutations. Common primitives such as headers and list rows depend directly on the current demo account; the separate `savita` feature directory embeds a person’s identity into presentation behaviour.

Introduce general user preferences and capabilities rather than hard-coding a named senior persona. Separate session/identity, domain data, derived views and local UI state. Keep the current components, but move mutations into explicit actions and selectors: record dose status, complete a task instance, reschedule an appointment, grant access and acknowledge an update. Derive Home, AI context and Medical ID through the same access-controlled selectors.

The model needs to distinguish the care recipient, acting user, assignee, reporter, payer, provider and notification recipient. A task currently has one `memberId`, which cannot express “Arjun is responsible for Rajiv’s appointment preparation.” Further required concepts include consent grants, document assets, timestamped observations with units/provenance, dose events, recurring task instances, zoned appointments, service quotes and delivery acknowledgements. These are product requirements, not a mandate to replace the stack.

There is no backend, authentication provider, durable database, push scheduler, document storage/OCR, health-source connector, payment provider, real-time video system or production AI service in the app. Adding an AI API alone would leave the core coordination problems unresolved.

**Cross-country requirements deserve explicit acceptance criteria.** An appointment should display when it happens in the recipient’s location and the organiser’s location, including daylight-saving changes. A local helper should be able to accept or decline a task. “No update received” must remain distinct from “everything is fine.” Sharing must name the recipient, scope and duration, with a way to withdraw it. A payer abroad and a care recipient locally need a clear booking ownership and cancellation model. Provider service areas, currencies, languages and qualifications need meaningful constraints. Country-specific legal and clinical obligations require their own scoped review before real deployment; this audit does not establish compliance.

**Accessibility and design assessment.** Selected token contrast calculations were 5.33:1 for secondary text on the background, 5.39:1 for tertiary text on the surface, and 5.51:1 for white text on blue. These are colour samples, not a screen-level accessibility pass. Text allows up to 200% scaling, and Savita’s preference applies a custom increase. Fixed-height buttons, large modal contents, two-column metric cards, long labels and shared screens need device-level verification. Many controls have labels, but coverage and interaction semantics are uneven. For example, completion controls are often buttons with checked state, and nested pressables need testing with assistive technology.

For the organiser, the first view should communicate changed information, an accountable next action and unresolved items. For the personal account, prioritise the immediate task, the next appointment and help. Preserve readable type and touch targets while reducing duplicated content. The four tabs plus central quick-add control create five persistent actions; whether Savita needs all five should be tested. A specific 44-point control is not by itself proof of a WCAG failure; touch size, spacing and real usability need evaluation together.

**Recommended delivery sequence.** This is a recommendation, not a commitment to build these changes during an audit.

| Stage | Scope | Completion criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Make the demonstration truthful | Correct names/counts/clock; stop inferred dose mutations; scope records/actions; label simulation; align compatible dependencies | The same event produces consistent state everywhere; no confirmation claims an unperformed action; malformed routes fail safely |
| 2. Prove one family coordination flow | Real organiser and recipient identities, invitation/consent, durable shared state, one uploaded record, one assigned action, confirmation and notification | Two devices in different countries complete the flow and retain it through restart; withdrawal of access removes access everywhere |
| 3. Make daily use dependable | Dose/task instances, actual readings, provenance, offline/retry handling, local time display, recipient language and accessibility | Missed updates, duplicates, retries and daylight-saving changes produce understandable and correct behaviour |

| 5. Prove professional fulfilment | Start with a bounded geography/service, actual availability, confirmation, service quote, cancellation and follow-up | A booking reaches a real verified professional and its delivery/cancellation is reflected for the family |

For the first usable version, prioritise a consented family circle, a clear shared update stream, records, a small set of care tasks, an appointment view and explicit confirmation. Keep the expansive marketplace, broad wellness categories, synthetic matching percentages, video consultation and complex goal features out of the critical path until that flow is dependable. Existing screens can remain as clearly marked demonstrations while this is developed.

**Commercial and operational questions are still open.** The interface suggests a family subscription and paid care sessions, but neither pricing nor acquisition economics is validated. The family subscription is a reasonable hypothesis to test after families show repeat use; provider payments introduce separate fulfilment responsibilities. A marketplace requires onboarding and verifying professionals, maintaining availability, handling failed visits/cancellations and support, and reconciling payments. None of that operational evidence exists in this repository. Rankings should have an understandable basis, particularly if commercial relationships later affect them.

**A useful validation plan.** Recruit a small set of families across countries, including an organiser, the parent/older relative and, where applicable, a local helper. As a proposed research starting point, five to eight family groups can reveal workflow failures; this is not a market-size or statistical claim. Observe a real recent care-coordination story before asking participants to use the product. Test adding a report, choosing who sees it, assigning a follow-up, marking completion, handling an unanswered request and arranging a visit across time zones. Include an older participant using their preferred language and accessibility settings.

Measure first successful shared action, acknowledgement/completion of agreed tasks, stale/unresolved items, second-week family participation, correction rate for explanations/logs and self-reported coordination burden. Do not equate app openings, AI message count or reduced calls with better care without understanding the outcome. No analytics instrumentation currently measures these product outcomes.

The remaining founder decisions are the first launch corridor and service area; who invites and pays; how consent and assisted use work; whether care fulfilment is an early offering or later expansion; and the exact outcome the first pilot must prove. The audience itself is now clear: **families trying to care for one another across countries, with the organiser abroad and the person receiving care both treated as first-class users.**
